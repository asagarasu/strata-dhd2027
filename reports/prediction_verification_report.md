## Tang → en

**HIGH: Arthur Waley** — ● **CONFIRMED**
- H. Mack Horton, "Making it Old: Premodern Japanese Poetry in English Translation," *Asia Pacific Translation and Intercultural Studies* 5:2 (2018), 110–204 (a study of Japanese, not Chinese, translation, but with a detailed section on Waley's method and reception). Horton describes Waley's "hard-headed philological method" and "unembellished accuracy," notes he was praised by critic Babette Deutsch for his unornamented, terse approach, and that he dismissed his predecessors' "very free versions" — establishing the reputational contrast between Waley-style literalism and looser competitors. https://doi.org/10.1080/23306343.2018.1500980
- General reference sources (Britannica, Wikipedia, dissertation abstracts) converge on Waley as the translator who "introduced ancient Chinese poetry to a wide public" via a "new form of free verse" prized for literalness, though at least one Chinese-language critical study ("Treason and Defect in Arthur Waley's English Translation…") pushes back on the completeness of his fidelity — a normal scholarly nuance, not a contradiction of the *reputation* claim, which concerns what Waley is known for, not whether he is flawless. https://davidpublisher.com/Public/uploads/Contribute/610229b379c07.pdf
- Note: my search focused on Waley's Japanese-verse reputation via Horton (the strongest single source found) plus general reference material for the Chinese-poetry side; a dedicated Sinological source (e.g., de Gruchy 2003, as flagged in the draft) was not separately pulled up in full text, so treat this as solidly-but-not-exhaustively confirmed.

**HIGH alt: Stephen Owen** — ● **CONFIRMED**
- Review, *The Poetry of Du Fu*, trans. Stephen Owen, ed. Owen/Kroll/Warner, *Project MUSE* (review of the De Gruyter edition). The review characterizes the six-volume, 3,000-page translation as furnished with "explanatory footnotes, scholarly endnotes... and a guide to major allusions," i.e., the "scholarly apparatus" claimed in the draft, and calls it the first complete translation of any single Chinese poet's oeuvre. https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/109/article/759138/summary
- Harvard Magazine, "Stephen Owen Translates the Complete Works of Chinese Poet Du Fu into English" (2016), confirms the same scale and scholarly framing. https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2016/04/complete-works-of-du-fu-in-translation

**LOW: Ezra Pound, *Cathay*** — ● **CONFIRMED**
- Royal Literary Fund, "Cathay 100," and Ploughshares, "The Modernist Revision of a Foreign Culture in Ezra Pound's *Cathay*," both document the well-known Fenollosa-notes errors (e.g., conflating two separate Li Bai poems into one, "tied"/"tired" misreadings) alongside *Cathay*'s canonical status as English poetry. Hugh Kenner is cited as reading *Cathay* "as if [the poems] were English poems," explicitly downgrading fidelity concerns as "deflections undertaken with open eyes" — precisely the beautiful-but-free archetype the draft claims. https://www.rlf.org.uk/posts/cathay-100/ ; https://pshares.org/blog/the-modernist-revision-of-a-foreign-culture-in-ezra-pounds-cathay/

**LOW alt: 许渊冲 (rhymed "三美" versions)** — ◐ **CONFIRMED**
- Academic surveys of 许渊冲's "三美" (three beauties) doctrine confirm the draft's framing precisely: he is grouped with the "rhyme/form-first" school against a "free verse, meaning-first" school, and is explicitly criticized in the literature for the theory "inevitably sacrificing the natural expression of language and poetry content in practice." This is a live, debated LOW candidate, exactly as flagged. Xia, "English Translation of Classical Chinese Poetry," *Orbis Litterarum* (2018), https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/wol1/doi/10.1111/oli.12184/fullpdf; additional Chinese-language surveys of 三美 theory confirm the same fault line (Hanspub and 维普 journal articles on 许渊冲's practice in Tang-poetry translation).
- The draft's caution about "tone... his stature in China invites reviewer heat" is well founded: sources treat him as a towering, honored figure (State Council recognition, Nobel-adjacent prestige claims in Chinese press) even while academically critiquing the rhyme-first method, so any write-up should keep the critique method-specific rather than personal.

## Haiku → en

**HIGH: R. H. Blyth** — ◐ **CONFIRMED**
- The Haiku Foundation's "Haiku Resources: A Scholar's Library of Haiku in English" states Blyth's four-volume *Haiku* is "recognized as the authoritative anthology of Japanese haiku" and that "no one has come close to the extraordinary amount of material he has published," though it notes some later commentators criticize his over-reliance on Zen interpretation. https://thehaikufoundation.org/juxta/juxta-1-1/haiku-resources-a-scholars-library-of-haiku-in-english/
- This supports "standard scholarly-respect rendering" but I did not find a source specifically confirming "direct, unrhymed, image-preserving" as the operative critical vocabulary (as opposed to general "authoritative"/"master of translation" language) — a minor gap, not a contradiction.

**LOW: Harold Stewart, *A Net of Fireflies*** — ● **CONFIRMED**
- Joseph P. Love, review of *A Net of Fireflies: Japanese Haiku and Haiku Paintings*, *Monumenta Nipponica* 18:1 (1963), 384–86 — a serious scholarly-journal review, confirming this is indeed a critically discussed case (I could only retrieve the citation/metadata, not the full review text via JSTOR, but its existence in a top Japan-studies journal corroborates that Stewart's rhymed-couplet method was taken seriously enough to warrant formal academic review). https://dept.sophia.ac.jp/monumenta/article/a-net-of-fireflies-japanese-haiku-and-haiku-paintings-with-verse-translations-and-an-essay-by-harold-stewart/
- Popular/secondary sources (Goodreads, Amazon reader commentary) independently and consistently describe Stewart's method as recasting every haiku into rhymed couplets, with the reception "markedly divided" and some readers calling the result "cutsie" children's-rhyme-like — consistent with, though not as authoritative as, the "textbook domesticator" framing in the draft.
- I was not able to locate the specific secondary-literature line naming Stewart as "the textbook domesticator" verbatim (e.g., in Kern's *Making Haiku Anglophone* or comparable); H. Mack Horton's 2018 *Asia Pacific Translation and Intercultural Studies* survey of Anglophone Japanese-poetry translation (cited above for Waley) does not treat Stewart specifically. Recommend softening the citation claim to "critically discussed as a rhymed-domesticating outlier" rather than asserting the exact phrase is in the literature.

## Sonnets → zh

**HIGH: 梁宗岱** — ◐ **CONFIRMED**
- 余光中 (Yu Guangzhong), quoted across multiple Chinese sources, on 梁宗岱's 1970 retranslation (published in 人民文学出版社's 1978 *莎士比亚全集* vol. 11): "梁氏译文对原文体会深入，诠释委婉……所入颇深，所出也颇纯" (Liang's translation shows deep comprehension of the original and nuanced interpretation — going in deep, coming out pure), calling it in effect the "best translation." http://tongchuan.kouyijia.com/lyqy/ldscbj.html ; https://www.chinawriter.com.cn/n1/2020/0901/c404063-31843839.html
- A comparative academic article on translations of Sonnet 29 (上海理工大学学报, jss.usst.edu.cn) independently states that 梁译 ("Liang's translation") "emphasizes faithful rendering of the original text" with "concise and elegant language," while noting some loss of emotional intensity — this is a genuine scholarly source (not just reader review) confirming the fidelity reputation. https://jss.usst.edu.cn/html/2019/4/20190406.htm
- A further comparative source states directly: "梁译和屠译最注重忠实原文，基本上每个词都能做到与原诗一一对应" (Liang's and Tu's translations most emphasize fidelity to the original, achieving near word-for-word correspondence with the original poem) — this is the clearest single confirmation of the HIGH claim. (Aggregated via web search of comparative reviews; original blog/note source, treat as a lead rather than a peer-reviewed citation.)

**LOW candidates (research gap, now filled):**

1. **辜正坤 (Gu Zhengkun)** — proposed LOW/domesticating-by-paraphrase candidate. **CONFIRMED as a genuine free/paraphrase-oriented outlier, though "elegant but flattening" needs a caveat.**
   - The same 上海理工大学学报 comparative article on Sonnet 29 states 辜译 ("Gu's translation") is "primarily interpretive rather than literal translation" (意译为主) and that it "deepens readers' affective experience through refined word choices," even calling his final couplet "a masterpiece of Chinese translation" — i.e., 辜's version is explicitly framed in scholarship as sacrificing literalness for polish/affect, matching the "smooth but domesticating" profile, though the same source treats this as an aesthetic virtue rather than a flaw. https://jss.usst.edu.cn/html/2019/4/20190406.htm
   - A second comparative source states directly: "辜译和晚译主要以意译为主，注重原诗整体意义传达" (Gu's and Wan[feng]'s translations are mainly paraphrase-oriented, focused on conveying the overall sense rather than exact wording) — corroborating from an independent source that 辜正坤 sits on the free/interpretive end against 梁/屠's literalism.
   - Caveat: I did not find a source explicitly using the phrase "elegant but flattened" (典雅而失真/削平) about Gu specifically — the sourced language is "意译" (paraphrase-oriented), which is close to but not identical to "flattening." Recommend the protocol entry cite it as "free/interpretive," not verbatim "flattened."

2. **梁实秋 (Liang Shiqiu)** — a stronger, more explicitly criticized LOW candidate not in the original draft's short-list, surfaced during this pass. **CONFIRMED, well-documented.**
   - Multiple Chinese sources converge: 梁实秋 completed the sonnets (and the entire Shakespeare corpus) in **prose-like form** ("散文化的翻译方式"), reportedly on Hu Shi's ("胡适") suggestion to "try translating in prose." Critics are quoted faulting this directly on formal grounds: "十四行诗是格律最严的西方诗歌体裁，不守格律的译文只能算'意译'，不足作译诗论" (the sonnet is one of the most metrically strict Western verse forms; a translation that disregards its meter can only be called 'paraphrase,' not a proper poetic translation) — line lengths in a single translated sonnet are reported to range from 10 to 17 characters, i.e., no attempt at formal regularity. This is a stronger, more explicitly sourced LOW candidate than any Gu Zhengkun citation I found, and I recommend the protocol consider substituting or adding 梁实秋 as the primary Sonnets→zh LOW nomination, with 辜正坤 as an alternate.
   - Note 梁实秋's translation was, however, praised in places for readability and faithfulness of *sense* even while criticized for form — so it is a "loose form / retains content" case rather than a "beautiful surface, false content" case; the fit with the draft's exact "elegant but flattened" framing is partial. It is nonetheless a well-documented, citable LOW-reputation candidate for the cluster.

**Sources to pull further** (recommended, not yet retrieved in full text): 余光中's actual preface/序言 to the 梁宗岱译集 reissue; 方平's 1950s critical essay on 屠岸's translation (referenced secondhand, not located directly); a dedicated translation-studies monograph on Chinese Shakespeare-sonnet retranslation history (the "台湾莎学研究情况综述" PDF surfaced in search but not fetched).

## Baudelaire → zh

**HIGH: 戴望舒 (partial corpus)** — ◐ **CONFIRMED**
- Multiple sources confirm 戴望舒's 《恶之花掇英》(1947, 怀正文化社) as the first Chinese translation of any Baudelaire, covering 24 poems only (partial corpus, as flagged in the draft) — confirming the coverage caveat is warranted. https://www.chinawriter.com.cn/n1/2021/0611/c404092-32128340.html
- On the fidelity/modernist-reputation side: one source states 戴望舒's rendering is preferred by some critics because "他的遣词更会体现诗句的想象力和浪漫色彩，注重韵律和节奏" (his diction better conveys the poem's imaginative and romantic color, with attention to rhythm and meter) — this is more a "poetic feel" reputation than strictly a "fidelity" reputation, and a scholarly source on his translation theory notes he held a functional-equivalence ("Nida-style") view of fidelity, i.e., fidelity to affective/emotional truth rather than literal wording. This nuances but does not contradict the draft's claim — his reputation is for modernist poetic sensibility and emotional fidelity, not word-for-word precision.

**HIGH: complete-edition "faithful standard" — 钱春绮 vs. 郭宏安 (research gap, now addressed):**
- Both hold strong scholarly reputations; neither has a clean, exclusive claim to "the" faithful-standard title, but the balance of evidence favors **钱春绮 (Qian Chunqi)** as the more consistently cited authoritative/standard reference edition:
  - 钱春绮 (1921–2010) was awarded China Translators Association's "资深翻译家" (senior translator) honorific and the Lu Xun Literary Prize; his Baudelaire translations are published by 人民文学出版社 under its "一流的原著、一流的译本、一流的译者" (first-rate original, first-rate translation, first-rate translator) editorial standard, and multiple secondary comparisons state "钱春绮的译本是较好的" (Qian's edition is the better one) among the extant complete/near-complete editions. https://baike.baidu.com/item/%E9%92%B1%E6%98%A5%E7%BB%AE/5644113 ; https://www.zhihu.com/question/21301548
  - **郭宏安 (Guo Hong'an)** is independently well-credentialed: a scholar who wrote a 140,000-character monograph *论〈恶之花〉* on Baudelaire before/alongside translating, and whose method is described as "贴近" (close/proximate), attentive to Baudelaire's rhyme schemes (交韵/随韵/抱韵) and to the "ugly-but-beautiful" aesthetic content (corpses, drunkards) rather than prettifying it — this is arguably a *stronger* claim to scholarly fidelity than Qian's, since it is explicitly theorized as fidelity to content/aesthetic intention, not just diction. https://news.pku.edu.cn/bdrw/137-117828.htm
  - **Verdict: UNRESOLVED/split.** The two editions have different but overlapping claims to "faithful standard" status — 钱春绮 more often invoked as the general-purpose authoritative complete edition; 郭宏安 more often invoked in fidelity-to-aesthetic-content terms and cited alongside his own critical scholarship. I recommend the protocol name both as co-anchors for the HIGH slot rather than picking one, or explicitly framing 钱 as the "standard reference" and 郭 as the "scholar-critic's edition."

**LOW candidate (research gap) — NOT successfully filled:**
- I was unable to find a complete/near-complete zh 《恶之花》 edition with a clearly documented "smooth/beautiful but domesticating" critical reputation comparable to the Sonnets→zh or Baudelaire→en LOW cases. Candidates considered and their status:
  - **陈敬容** (translated ~40 poems, 九叶派 poet): one scholarly source documents her taking real interpretive liberties (e.g., rendering "désespoir" as "失望的悲哀" to soften despair into melancholy, reframing darkness as a positive/liberating force in "La Musique") — this is a genuine documented case of meaning-shifting license, but it is framed positively in the source as poetic insight ("诗人更懂诗"), not critiqued as a flaw, and her corpus is partial (like 戴望舒's), not a complete edition.
  - **郑克鲁** translated only a partial selection (19 poems in one recent illustrated edition) — insufficient as a "complete edition" LOW candidate, and no critical "smooth but unfaithful" commentary was found.
  - No source characterized any complete zh Baudelaire edition as the "elegant/rhymed but domesticating" counterpart to 钱春绮/郭宏安.
- **Recommendation: retract or downgrade this nomination slot to ○ (thin/deferred)** pending either (a) a search pass specifically in CNKI/中国知网 for translation-critique articles unavailable to open web search, or (b) accepting 陈敬容 as a partial-corpus LOW candidate with the caveat that her interpretive licenses are documented but not conventionally framed as faults.

## Baudelaire → en

**HIGH: Richard Howard** — ◐ **CONFIRMED, with an important complication.**
- Howard's *Les Fleurs du Mal* (1982) won the American Book Award for Translation and was called by *The Nation* a translation that will "long stand as definitive." This supports the "praised" half of the claim. https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/entertainment/books/1982/08/01/fresh-cut-flowers-of-evil/b6ef6e01-f91f-4e48-bca8-c99848401b12/ ; Project MUSE review, https://muse.jhu.edu/article/460116/summary
- However, the *same body of criticism* found in this search explicitly disputes the "fidelity" framing: reviewers note that "literalists notice absolute fidelity is sometimes sacrificed for poetic ends: Howard misses important effects, at times leaves out an image or allusion, occasionally rearranges the order of lines, inserts extraneous names," with at least one academic review calling some renderings "unrecognizable" against the original.
- **This is a partial contradiction worth flagging explicitly**: Howard's *public/prize reputation* is for being the modern "definitive" translation, but his reputation *among literalist critics specifically* is decidedly mixed on fidelity — he is closer to "the prestige unrhymed version that takes more liberties than assumed" than to a clean philological-fidelity HIGH case like Aggeler. Recommend the protocol either soften the HIGH claim to "prestige/definitive reputation" rather than "fidelity," or replace Howard with Aggeler as the primary HIGH anchor and keep Howard as a complicated secondary example.

**HIGH alt: William Aggeler** — ◐ **CONFIRMED**
- Aggeler's 1954 translation (reprinted with Frank Pearce Sturm's introduction) is independently characterized as "often literal, almost prose-like," and his edition is also noted for scholarly value in including the six poems banned from the 1857 edition in both French and English — consistent with the "close literal verse" characterization in the draft. https://www.everand.com/book/351497839/

**LOW: Millay/Dillon, *Flowers of Evil*** — ● **CONFIRMED**
- Millay herself is on record acknowledging some of the Millay/Dillon versions "are not so much translations as adaptations." Contemporary and retrospective reviews praise it as "one of the most persuasive renditions" of Baudelaire's "opulence" while explicitly framing it as a free, English-poeticized recreation rather than a strict translation — this is as clean a confirmation of the "famously gorgeous, famously free" LOW archetype as exists in the cluster. Sources aggregated from Penguin Random House and 4Columns coverage of the New York Review Books reissue.

**LOW alt: Roy Campbell** — ◐ **CONFIRMED**
- Campbell's translation is described in the secondary literature as strong poetry in its own right, with critical concern specifically that "his own muscular style might overpower that of Baudelaire's" — directly matching the draft's "rhymed, muscular, liberty-taking" characterization. https://thepointmag.com/criticism/king-of-a-rainy-country/ ; https://hudsonreview.com/2022/01/terrible-baudelaire/
