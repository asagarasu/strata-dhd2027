# Reputation verification: Shigeyoshi Obata (1922) and Ayscough/Lowell (1921)

Compiled 2026-07-05, via web search only (no access to paywalled full texts
beyond what's reported below — several key sources were behind paywalls I
could not get past; this is flagged explicitly wherever it applies rather
than papered over). This report does **not** classify either translator's
reputation as HIGH or LOW. It reports what documented sources say and lets
the reader weigh it. Where evidence was thin, contradictory, or simply
unreachable, that is stated plainly rather than filled in with inference.

---

## Method and honesty note up front

I could get *citations* for several relevant pieces of peer-reviewed
scholarship, but was blocked (HTTP 403, paywall) from reading the actual
text of some of them — notably a 1924 Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society
review and a Comparative Literature: East & West article. In those cases
I report the citation and what limited information (title, abstract
fragments found via search, secondary mentions) I could establish, and say
explicitly that I could not verify the substance beyond that. I am not
guessing at what a paywalled review says based on its title.

I deliberately excluded reader-review sites (Goodreads, forums, blogs)
as primary evidence per the task's instructions, with one exception noted
below (a personal blog) which I flag explicitly as non-scholarly and
weight accordingly — I did not treat it as if it were scholarship.

## (a) Shigeyoshi Obata, *The Works of Li Po, the Chinese Poet* (1922)

**What I found, with sources:**

1. **Obata's own preface** (primary source, not independent scholarship —
   flagging the distinction) stakes an explicit fidelity claim against
   his predecessors. Per his book's introduction (quoted via search
   excerpts of the actual text, cross-checked against the archive.org
   copy fetched for this corpus): Obata criticizes Pound's method as
   containing "extravagant errors," and specifically charges that
   Pound's "The River Song" conflates two separate Li Bai poems into one
   composite ("gathers two different poems of Li Po into one,
   incorporating the title of the second piece in the body of his
   baffling conglomeration"). He also flags a specific mistranslation in
   Waley's account of Li Po's death. This is Obata asserting his own
   greater fidelity — a claim, not a verdict from an outside party — and
   should be weighted as such.

2. **Eugene Chen Eoyang, "The Case of the Mistaken 'Jeweled
   Staircase,'"** in *East-West Symbioses: The Reconciliation of
   Opposites* (Newcastle: Cambridge Scholars Publishing, 2019), pp.
   75-82 (also circulated as an article in *Comparative Literature:
   East & West*, Vol. 20, No. 1, 2014) —
   https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/25723618.2014.12015468
   (abstract page also 403'd for me; citation and content summary
   below come from secondary search results describing the article,
   not the full text itself, so treat this as lower-confidence than a
   direct quote).
   What it argues, per available summaries: that the standard reading
   of Li Bai's 玉階怨 (which has the palace woman retreat indoors after
   watching from the steps — "she turns within, lets fall the crystal
   curtain" in Obata's wording, essentially the same in Pound's) may be
   a **mistaken interpretation inherited across the whole translation
   tradition**, with Eoyang arguing for a reading where the woman
   remains outside throughout, which he says better fits the poem's
   logic of steadfastness. If this is right, it is a documented critique
   that applies to Obata's and Pound's (and by extension most
   translators') rendering of this specific poem alike — not a critique
   that distinguishes Obata's fidelity from Pound's, but a flag that the
   inherited "she goes inside" reading both share may itself be an error
   that predates and outlasts both of them.

3. **Reception/influence, not fidelity**: Obata's translations were used
   as the textual basis for musical settings by composer Harry Partch
   (*Seventeen Lyrics by Li Po*) and by Constant Lambert — documented
   cultural influence, found via multiple search hits, but this speaks
   to the book's reach/durability, not to translation accuracy.

4. **What I could NOT find**: a peer-reviewed, contemporaneous (1922-23)
   scholarly review of Obata's book with an independent verdict on its
   fidelity (e.g., in JAOS, Modern Philology, or similar). I searched
   specifically and did not surface one accessible to me. This is a real
   gap — it means Obata's translation-quality reputation, as best I can
   establish it, rests mostly on (i) his own claims in his preface, (ii)
   one specific interpretive critique (Eoyang) that isn't really about
   him distinctively, and (iii) general, non-scholarly commentary (see
   next point) — not on a body of sustained academic assessment I was
   able to locate.

5. **Non-scholarly source, flagged as such**: a personal blog
   (frankhudson.org, "The Parlando Project," run by a non-academic
   musician/writer) asserts Obata's translations are more accurate than
   Pound's and "missed [fewer] certain cultural nuances," but cites no
   scholarship for this beyond quoting Obata's own preface. I am
   reporting that this claim exists on the open web, explicitly **not**
   as evidence of documented scholarly reputation — it shouldn't be
   weighted the same as a peer-reviewed source, and I want that
   distinction to survive into however this gets used.

**Bottom line for (a):** the evidence I could gather does not clearly
establish an academic-consensus fidelity reputation for Obata one way or
the other. What exists is: Obata's own explicit self-positioning as more
faithful than Pound (a claim by an interested party); one real, but
narrow and shared-with-others, interpretive critique from Eoyang; and
documented downstream artistic use. I did not find a countervailing
scholarly source calling Obata's translations inaccurate, but I also did
not find an independent scholarly source calling them accurate — the gap
is in independent verification either way, not in balanced-but-mixed
evidence.

## (b) Amy Lowell & Florence Ayscough, *Fir-Flower Tablets* (1921)

**What I found, with sources:**

1. **A. M. Blackman and Lionel Giles, review of *Fir-Flower Tablets*,**
   *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society*, Vol. 56, No. 2 (April 1924),
   pp. 332-333.
   https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/firflower-tablets-poems-translated-from-the-chinese-by-florence-ayscough-english-versions-by-amy-lowell-8-5-xcv-227-pp-london-constable-1922/959A27D9C59E32C093A3EA107BAAD05A
   This is a real, contemporaneous, peer-level review — Lionel Giles was
   a professional British Museum sinologist (son of Herbert Giles,
   compiler of the Wade-Giles system and himself a well-known
   translator of Chinese classics), so this reviewer pairing carries
   real sinological authority. **I could not access the review's actual
   text or verdict** — Cambridge Core returned only the citation with no
   abstract ("an abstract is not available for this content"), and I
   have no way to report what Blackman and Giles actually concluded. I
   am flagging its existence and citation as a lead for a human with
   institutional access, not claiming to know its content.

2. **Yan Liu, "The Imagists' Misinterpretation of Classical Chinese
   Poetics,"** *Comparative Literature: East & West*, Vol. 12, No. 1
   (2010), pp. 37-51.
   https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/25723618.2010.12015387
   (author affiliated with Guangdong University of Foreign Studies, per
   search metadata). Again, full text was 403'd for me. What I could
   establish from search-result fragments and the title itself: the
   article groups Pound's *Cathay* and Ayscough/Lowell's *Fir-Flower
   Tablets* together as instances of Imagist-era "misinterpretation" of
   classical Chinese poetics, citing structural losses (rhyme scheme,
   tonal pattern) common to translating classical Chinese verse into
   free-verse English, and Pound's reliance on Fenollosa's imperfect
   transcriptions. I could not confirm from what I read whether the
   article treats Ayscough/Lowell's specific errors as comparably severe
   to Pound's, less severe, or worse — the title promises a critical
   stance toward both, but I don't have the substance to say more than
   that without over-claiming.

3. **Ayscough's character-splitting method — the specific technique
   named in the task**: Lowell's own preface (primary source, already
   saved to raw/firflower_ayscough_lowell_1921.txt in this corpus)
   explicitly *disclaims* heavy reliance on this technique: "The
   analysis of characters has been employed very rarely, and only when
   the text seemed to lean on the allusion for an added vividness or
   zest... In only one case in the book have I permitted myself to use
   an adjective not inherent in the character." So by Lowell's own
   account the method critics most associate with this book was used
   sparingly, not systematically.
   Independent commentary on the *method itself* (as opposed to this
   specific book) comes from Ken Knabb's annotated bibliography
   "Gateway to the Vast Realms" (Bureau of Public Secrets, 2004) —
   https://www.bopsecrets.org/gateway/literature/chinese-japanese.htm —
   which is a widely-circulated, well-informed reader's-guide-style
   bibliography, not peer-reviewed academic scholarship, so I'm
   weighting it as informed-commentary rather than scholarship. Its
   exact statement, important to get right: it is about **Ayscough's
   later, separate Tu Fu translations** (not *Fir-Flower Tablets*
   itself): "Ayscough's literal character-by-character translations are
   sometimes helpful, but sometimes misleading when (following the
   dubious notion propounded in the Pound-Fenollosa essay 'The Chinese
   Written Character as a Medium for Poetry') she expands the meaning of
   a character based on its supposed etymology." This directly names
   the same critique later leveled at Fenollosa/Pound's ideogrammic
   method (that Chinese characters were treated as more purely
   pictographic/etymologically transparent than they are — a point
   sinologists including James J. Y. Liu and George A. Kennedy have made
   about the Fenollosa/Pound essay specifically), and Knabb explicitly
   connects Ayscough's method to that same "dubious notion." But because
   this citation is about a different book by the same translator using
   the same method, I'm flagging the scope gap rather than silently
   applying it to *Fir-Flower Tablets*.

4. **Lindsay Shen, *Knowledge Is Pleasure: Florence Ayscough in
   Shanghai*** (Hong Kong: Hong Kong University Press, 2016) — a serious
   academic biography (published in a university press RAS-Shanghai
   series, indexed on Project MUSE and Oxford Scholarship Online). I
   could only retrieve marketing-style search-result summaries
   describing Ayscough generally as "one of the most respected
   interpreters of Chinese culture of the early 20th century" whose
   "modernist translations of the classical poets still command
   respect" — I was not able to get past these summaries into the
   book's actual critical content, so I can't confirm whether the book
   itself substantively defends or qualifies *Fir-Flower Tablets*'
   fidelity, or is simply using respectful framing typical of
   biography-jacket copy. Flagging this as unverified beyond the
   secondhand summary.

5. **What the collaboration structurally means for fidelity, undisputed
   and stated plainly by the translators themselves**: Ayscough (who
   lived in China, held an honorary position at the Royal Asiatic
   Society's North China Branch, and read Chinese) produced literal
   transliterations, word-by-word glosses, and prose paraphrases; Lowell
   (who "knew no Chinese," by her own repeated statement) versified from
   those materials into English free verse. This two-stage,
   crib-plus-poet structure is the same basic shape as Pound working
   from Fenollosa's notes — i.e., neither of this corpus's non-Waley
   translators (Pound, or Lowell/Ayscough) had a single person reading
   Chinese and writing the English poem directly; Obata is the odd one
   out in this respect, since he read Chinese and wrote the English
   himself.

**Bottom line for (b):** there is a real, citable, contemporaneous
peer-level review (Giles/Blackman, JRAS 1924) and at least one modern
academic article (Liu, 2010) that engage critically with this book or its
milieu, but I could not get past their citations to their actual verdicts
— that's a genuine access gap, not a finding of "no criticism exists." The
specific character-splitting critique the task asked about is
documented, but the clearest citable version of it (Knabb) is about
Ayscough's *other* (Tu Fu) translations using the same method, and is
informed commentary rather than peer-reviewed scholarship; Lowell's own
preface claims the technique was used only rarely in *this* book. I did
not find a source stating outright that *Fir-Flower Tablets* is
inaccurate, nor one stating outright that it is faithful — again, the gap
is in what I could verify, not evidence of consensus in either direction.
